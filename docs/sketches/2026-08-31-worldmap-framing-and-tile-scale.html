World map framing explorer

What is being decided: the camera and fog numbers for the out-of-battle world map — a painted region PNG laid on a ground plane in the same Node3D world the battle uses, viewed from above at a fixed yaw, the far distance washing out into haze. Specifically pitch, vertical FOV, camera height, fog band, ground curvature, world-units-per-map-pixel, and the one that decides an art spec rather than a camera setting: whether the region PNG is drawn plain top-down or pre-stretched vertically to survive the perspective squash. It also carries the render-scale and filtering comparison, on the understanding that on this project the sparkle and pixel noise are part of the look rather than a defect to remove.

Settled. The clean console capture has no horizon — the map runs off the top of the frame and the fog is a mild distance wash that never completes on screen — which ruled out every framing with a fogged horizon line and put pitch well above fov/2. Pitch 60 / FOV 25 follows from the reference's ~3:1 near-to-far depth ratio. Scale came from counting 16 px tiles rather than judging: ~53 across the bottom edge at ~7.5 buffer px each, the ground minified ~2.1×. Pre-stretching the art was rejected (only 1.15× at this pitch). Sprites are drawn at fixed screen size, not billboarded. These numbers were then ported to GDScript in WorldMapCameraRig and reproduce this page independently — 53.26 tiles, 7.21 px/tile, ratio 2.906, 155 tiles of region needed — so two implementations agree. The largest thing this page found is not a camera number at all: a region must be tiles_across × ratio wide, so the reference framing needs a ~155-tile region and the 48-tile map supplied here can only fill ~16 tiles across.

Drag to pan the camera. Load your own region PNG — the built-in map is a schematic placeholder, not art, and judging fog or scale against it will mislead you. The file is read locally in the browser and never leaves this machine.

Readout

What the knobs mean in Godot terms

  • Pitch → Camera3D.rotation_degrees.x = -pitch, yaw fixed at 0. Yaw is deliberately not a knob: a painted map has one baked light direction and upright icons, so rotating it breaks both, and a fixed yaw is what lets roads and cities be composited into the PNG instead of being separate meshes.
  • Vertical FOV → Camera3D.fov (vertical, since keep_aspect defaults to KEEP_HEIGHT). Pitch and FOV are not independent here, and the constraint is the first thing to internalise: the horizon is only on screen when pitch < fov/2. The reference is comfortably on the other side of that line — no horizon, no sky, the map simply leaves the top of the frame — so the first decision is not "where does the horizon sit" but "is there one at all", and the reference says no. Given that, the number that describes the look is the near-to-far depth ratio across the frame, which is what sets how strongly the world converges; the readout reports the bottom-of-screen ray angle (pitch + fov/2) alongside it. Pitch alone tells you nothing, and neither does FOV alone — they trade against each other at constant ratio.
  • Curvature k → the ground is y = -k·d² where d is forward distance. A vertex shader on a subdivided PlaneMesh. At k = 0 the plane is flat and the horizon sits where the maths puts it; raising k pulls the horizon nearer and makes the world fall away like a globe. Note it changes the fog band you need, because the far ground stops existing sooner.
  • Fog → do this in the same custom shader as everything else. Godot's environment fog is skipped by unshaded materials, so trying to drive this from WorldEnvironment will fight you and will also couple the map's fog to whatever environment the battle scene wants.
  • Off-map void shows where the PNG ends. Whatever colour you pick, the goal is to tune fog so the void is never visible — that is the measurement this sketch exists to produce, and it converts directly into how much margin the art needs past the playable area.

The pre-stretch decision

A map drawn plain top-down (like a paper map) gets vertically compressed by the pitch — a circular lake reads as an ellipse only sin(pitch) as tall as it is wide in the near field, and flatter than that toward the horizon. Two honest answers. Accept it — this is what the console reference does, and it reads fine because the eye reads the squash as ground rather than as distortion. Or pre-stretch the PNG vertically by 1 / sin(pitch) so near-field shapes read round.

Decided: accept the squash. The steep reference pitch is what settles it. At 60° the compensating stretch is only 1.15× — small enough that pre-stretching buys almost nothing while still locking the art to one pitch forever and costing a non-square source texture. It is worth knowing the earlier shallow framings were a different story: at 22° the compensation is 2.67×, so a 2048 px wide region would have needed a 5500 px tall source. That framing is gone, and the argument for pre-stretching went with it. Still tell whoever draws the map that vertical distances compress by ~13%, so anything that must read as a specific shape (a circular lake, a city footprint) is worth checking here before the art is finished. The button and the wide slider remain so the alternative can be seen rather than argued about.

The filtering decision

Read this before reaching for the filter dropdown. On this project the pixel noise, the sparkle and the optical artefacts are wanted — they are the texture of the genre, not defects to be engineered away. So the question is not "how do we stop the shimmer", it is "how do we render so the shimmer reads as texture rather than as a bug", and the answer to that is mostly render scale, not filtering. A low internal buffer makes aliasing coherent and chunky; a native buffer with nearest makes it fine-grained and twitchy, which is the version that reads as broken. The default here is 0.50 for that reason.

The mechanism, for when you do want to reason about it: each screen row of this rig is a constant world depth, so the horizontal and vertical texel footprints differ enormously — that difference is the anisotropy, and it is why a plain mip chain looks like mud here while nearest looks like static. Turn on Drift forward before judging any of it; a still frame will not show you sparkle at all. The Same framing, filtered preset is there to show what the clean version costs you, as a comparison rather than a recommendation.

Measured while building this, and it changes the priority: the three filter modes produce identical output across the near two-thirds of the frame, because at any sane units-per-map-pixel the near ground is magnified — one map pixel covers several screen pixels — and magnification has no mip level to choose. They only diverge in a band in the mid-distance where the texture starts minifying. With the default fog band that whole band is already 50–90% fog, so the fog is doing most of the anti-aliasing and the filter setting is a much smaller decision than it looks. The readout line to trust is worst visible: if it stays at or below 1 texel/px, the ground is magnified everywhere you can actually see and nearest is correct — keep the crisp pixels. Push the fog out and it climbs immediately, which is the real reason to keep the fog band tight.

Tiles, sprites, and the thing that decides the zoom

The map is 16×16 tiles and so are the sprites, and that pins the scale far better than any amount of eyeballing the camera. In the reference — a 256×224 frame — a 16 px tile lands at about 7.5 frame pixels at the bottom of the screen. The ground is minified roughly 2× even at its nearest point, which puts ~34 tiles across the bottom edge. This canvas is 16:9 rather than 8:7, so the equivalent framing is ~53 tiles; turn on the tile grid and count them. Getting this wrong by 3× is the difference between "a world map" and "standing in a field", and it is invisible until you count.

Two consequences worth stating separately, because they are different decisions:

  • Framing is tiles across the frame. It is set by camera height against units-per-map-pixel, and it is resolution-independent.
  • Pixel density is buffer pixels per tile. It is set by render scale alone. The reference's 7.5 needs a ~384-wide buffer at this framing; a native 960-wide buffer gives 18 and the ground stops being minified at all, which loses the sparkle along with the look. This is the concrete reason render scale, not filtering, is the lever.

Sprites do not shrink with distance in the reference — units at the top of the frame are no smaller than those at the bottom, and all of them are drawn far larger than a ground tile at the same depth. They are blitted at native pixel size, not billboarded in world space. The sprite mode toggle shows both; fixed is the reference behaviour and world is the naive 3D answer, which shrinks the far party to a third of the near one. Worth deciding early: fixed screen size keeps the party readable and retro-correct, but it means the party is not really "in" the scene, and anything that must sit convincingly on the ground — a shadow, a road highlight touching its feet — has to be positioned in world space even though the sprite is not.

Not modelled here
