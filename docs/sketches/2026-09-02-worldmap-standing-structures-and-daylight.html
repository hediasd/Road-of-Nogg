Standing structures on temp2

Kept 2026-09-02. This is the sketch that settled how structures stand up on the world map, and it is preserved because three of its findings were reached by looking and would be expensive to re-derive: that the vertical squash is not cos(pitch), that a shadow must be a solid silhouette sheared in map space, and that a lamp should withhold the night rather than add light. All three shipped; the reasoning is docs/WORLDMAP_DESIGN.md sections 9 and 10.

One thing here deliberately did not ship: the Additive glow lamp mode. It is kept only as the comparison that makes the subtractive choice legible — seeing warm light painted on top, inventing colours the seven-colour palette does not contain, is what shows why every shipped mode withholds the night instead.

temp2 has nine structures painted into it — seven houses and two towers. This lifts them out of the ground layer, patches the ground where they were, and stands them back up as billboarded sprites under a day/night sun that casts real directional shadows. Camera opens on the saved Curved Close framing at 09:30.

Why this is narrower than the earlier 2.5D attempt, and why that matters. That one tried to stand up everything — mountains, trees, the lot — and failed because it cut sprites on the tile grid and turned organic blobs into cubes. Structures are different: small, rectangular, and made of three colours nothing else on the map uses, so they are unambiguous to find. Solid boxes and cylinders were tried on top of this and dropped; the sprite is the thing worth keeping.

Corrected while building this. These sprites are front elevations — a house is a teal upper wall with three windows above a white lower wall with an orange door, and there is no roof in the image at all. That is exactly why standing them up works: they are already drawn from the angle a standing quad shows them at. The other correction: the door is orange, a colour the map uses elsewhere, so the colour key misses it — patching only the keyed pixels left the door behind on the ground as a smudge. The bounding box is the sprite, so that is what gets patched.

What billboarding is actually fixing

A sprite stood upright in the world is a vertical quad, and a vertical quad seen from a pitched-down camera is squashed. The obvious guess is that the squash is cos p, one number for the whole frame. It is not, and the readout in this sketch is what caught it. Working the projection through, a sprite of world height h and width W standing at ground distance f projects to

hpx / wpx = (h/W) × f / (D − h sin p), where D = H sin p + f cos p is the view depth of its foot.

So the squash is f / D: 0 at the camera's own feet, rising to 1 / cos p far away, and passing through exactly cos p at the centre of the frame and nowhere else. On the Curved Close framing it measures 0.33 at the near edge and 0.76 at the far edge — a 2.3× swing inside a single frame. A house at the bottom of the screen is squashed more than twice as hard as one at the top.

That is the fact everything else follows from, and it rules out the fix I reached for first. The sketch exposes three knobs so they can be judged apart.

λ — face the camera

The textbook billboard. The quad's up-axis rotates from world-up (0,1,0) toward the camera's own up-axis, which in world space is (0, cos p, −sin p). Put A and B for that axis into the identity above and they come out A = 1, B = 0, so hpx/wpx = h/W — painted proportions exactly, at every distance and every pitch, with no correction term at all. That, and not "it cancels cos p", is the real reason λ is the robust answer.

The cost is a lie about footprint. The camera's up-axis leans into the scene, so a fully-facing sprite tips its top away from the viewer by h sin p. A tower 1.5 tiles tall at 58.5° has its roof sitting 1.28 tiles behind its own foot, covering ground it is not standing on. Behind is much cheaper than in front — a tall object occludes what is behind it anyway — but on a map where the player walks a road past that tower, it is a tile and a quarter of hidden road.

g — proportion gain

The fix that keeps the quad honestly vertical: scale its world height until the projection lands on painted proportions anyway. Because the identity is closed-form it inverts exactly — h = h₀D / (AD + ybB + h₀B) — so this is a solve, not a fudge, and g blends linearly from none of it to all of it.

What I got wrong first, and it is worth stating plainly: I applied a single 1/cos p to every sprite, on the belief that the squash was one number for the whole frame. Measured, that left the near house at h/w 0.931 and the far one at 1.291 against a painted 0.875 — over-corrected at the top of the screen, under-corrected at the bottom, wrong by up to 48%. The gain has to be per sprite, from its own distance. The readout's spread row is that error: it reads 0 only when proportions actually hold in depth.

What it costs: the object is genuinely taller in the world than its art implies, and by a factor that changes as the camera moves. With nothing else 3D in the scene that costs nothing. It starts to matter the moment two structures can occlude each other.

And here is where the two converge — completely. I expected them to land close and wrote "to within a term". Measured, every sprite corner agrees to 0.00 px at every pitch tested. They are not similar, they are the same rectangle, and by construction: the base and the width are identical in both, the solve forces the same h/w, and a rectangle is nothing more than that. They differ only in what they claim about world space — λ leans a correctly-sized quad, g keeps an oversized one upright — and neither claim is observable while sprites are the only 3D thing in the scene. So this is not a choice between two looks. It is a choice about which lie you would rather be holding when something else 3D eventually shows up, and λ is the cheaper one to hold: it needs no per-object solve, and it is one line in a vertex shader.

µ — follow the curve

This one is about the second half of the question, and the honest answer is that it is nearly a no-op. The ground is bent by y = −k d², so its surface tips away from the camera by atan(2kd); µ rotates the sprite's up-axis onto that local normal so it stands perpendicular to the ground it is actually on. With yaw pinned and the sprite an axis-aligned screen rectangle, though, tipping it has no visual signature except a height change — and any proportion correction removes exactly that. So µ is only visible at g < 1, and at g = 1 it does nothing you can see. Left in the panel because that is worth being able to check rather than take on trust.

Which means curvature was never the hard half. It reaches sprites through their base position, which the projection already handles correctly, and through a height change any correction absorbs. The one thing it genuinely broke is below.

Curvature was also deforming the sprites, and should not have been

A real defect this turned up. The projection applies the curve drop k d² per point, using each point's own distance. That is right for the ground, which is the curved surface. It is wrong for anything standing on it: the moment λ or µ moves a sprite's top to a different distance than its foot, the two ends take different drops and the sprite is sheared by the curve rather than carried by it. Sprites now project rigidly — the whole quad takes the drop belonging to its base. This never showed before only because a world-vertical sprite's top and foot sit at the same distance, so the bug had nothing to bite on.

How the structures are found — and why it will not last

No cleverness and no authored data. temp2 has a seven-colour palette, and three of those colours — near-white 245,255,245, near-black 2,21,0 and dark teal 11,105,106 — appear nowhere except on structures. Keying those, then taking connected components, finds exactly nine things and sorts them into two shapes by aspect ratio alone: seven 8×7 houses and two 8×13 towers. The tower's bottom row is a painted ground shadow, so it is dropped from the standing sprite and patched out of the ground.

Asked whether this generalises, I measured it instead of guessing, and the answer is no. Running the identical detector over the other region tells the whole story:

 temp2temp
palette7 colours1080 colours
components found9302
distinct box shapes210
median component50 px1 px
components of ≤4 px0 (0%)283 (94%)

On temp the same rule returns 302 blobs of which 94% are four pixels or smaller — it has latched onto dithering noise, because temp is anti-aliased and no colour in it is exclusive to anything. The nine-out-of-nine result is a property of that one map's palette, not a method. It happens to work; it does not generalise.

Trees are worse, and for a different reason

Buildings are at least made of pigment nothing else uses. A tree is made of the same pigment as the vegetation it stands in, so no key can separate them — not a better threshold, not a smarter one. Bucketing every green pixel on temp by its green channel shows one continuous population with no gap anywhere:

green channel80-9596-111112-127128-143144-159160-175176-191192-207
pixels9k34k26k14k24k236k123k12k

If tree-green were separable there would be a trough between two humps. There is one hump. Any threshold that catches the trees catches a slab of grass with them, and temp2 has no trees at all to tune against — its seven colours are sea, sand, grass, orange, and the three building colours.

So: do not detect. Author it.

The recommendation this measurement leads to is that structures should not be painted into the ground PNG in the first place. Give a region a second image the same size — a prop layer, transparent except where props sit — and paint the ground complete underneath it. Then extraction is exact rather than inferred, it costs nothing at runtime, it works for trees and anything else, and the ground patch problem disappears entirely because there is nothing to patch: the artist painted what is under the house. It is also less work for the artist than keeping a palette disjoint by hand.

The colour key stays useful as a bootstrap — it is how temp2 works today without anyone drawing a second layer — but it should be understood as scaffolding for one map, not the pipeline. Everything else in this sketch (the billboard maths, the shadows, the ground patch) is indifferent to where the sprite list came from.

Daylight and cast shadows

The shadow of a standing sprite is its silhouette sheared onto the ground along the sun: the foot edge stays where it is, the top edge lands h · cot(elevation) away in the direction opposite the sun, which makes a parallelogram drawn as two affine triangles. Silhouette, not rectangle — it costs the same and it is the difference between a tree casting a tree-shaped shadow and a tree casting a crate.

The sun stays on the camera's own side of the map, so every shadow is thrown away from the viewer. Shadows in front cover the ground the player is walking into and read as grime; shadows behind read as depth.

It sweeps a narrow arc, not the full east-to-west semicircle. Swinging the whole 180° puts the sun on the horizon due east at dawn, and a shadow cast from due east points due west — straight across the screen with no northward component at all. Those were the sideways streaks. Holding the arc inside ±52° of north keeps every shadow leaning away while still sweeping visibly:

clock06:3008:0009:3011:0012:3014:0015:3017:00
northward reach1.100.840.660.550.540.610.771.00
degrees off north48352294173043

Northward reach never goes negative and the lean tops out at 48°, against a full 90° — a shadow pointing due sideways — before. The direction still turns through the day, which is what carries the reading of what time it is.

The shadow is a trapezoid, not a parallelogram. The foot edge keeps the caster's own width; the tip edge is Shadow spread times wider. Physically that is the penumbra widening with distance from what casts it — only the contact edge is truly sharp. Practically it is what stops the shadow reading as a dash: a shadow lying flat and receding north is foreshortened by the camera far harder than the upright sprite beside it, so one exactly as wide as the house disappears into a line.

The silhouette is solid, not the sprite's alpha. Transparent pixels inside a structure — the tower's open belfry, the gap beside a doorway — must not punch holes in its shadow, because a real building has a solid door and glazed windows and light does not pour through the middle of it. Background is found by flooding in from the bounding box edge, four-connected, and everything the flood cannot reach is solid whatever colour it was. Taking the alpha directly gave the tower a shadow with a hole through it.

The sun keeps two elevations, on purpose. One is honest and goes to zero at both ends of the day — it drives the light colour, the lamps, and how strongly the shadow reads. The other never drops below Lowest sun, and it is the one the shadow geometry uses. cot(elevation) runs away as the sun touches the horizon, and a shadow twenty tiles long and one tile wide reads as a scratch on the lens rather than as a building. Measured against a house that is 1.00 tile wide and 0.88 tall:

Lowest sun10°18°22°27°32°40°
longest shadow3.502.602.111.681.381.03
at noon0.470.470.470.470.470.47

Default is 27°: the shadow runs from 0.47 tiles at noon to 1.68 at the day's edges, a 3.6× swing that is plainly legible as time passing without ever going thin. Holding the geometric elevation in a flattering band costs nothing readable, because "what time is it" comes from the direction the shadows point, not their length.

Three decisions worth surfacing rather than burying:

  • Shadows use the sprite's true height, not its billboard height. The billboard correction is a screen-space trick to keep proportions readable. Letting it into the lighting would swing every shadow by 2× when the gain is switched on — a rendering choice leaking into what is meant to be a physical cue.
  • Shadows are accumulated opaque and composited once. Drawn individually at partial alpha, two shadows crossing would darken where they overlap, which is wrong: ground in shadow twice over is just ground in shadow.
  • The geometric sun never sets. Holding elevation above a floor is physically wrong, and it is the reason shadows stay readable at 06:05. The alternative is a sunrise nobody wants to look at.

The light tint is a model, not a measurement — unlike the projection maths in this file there is nothing to check it against except whether it reads. It is applied to the ground, the fog, the void and the sprites together; tinting the ground alone and leaving a pale teal horizon behind a midnight-blue map is what makes a day cycle look broken. The Light tint slider takes it back out so the shadows can be judged on their own.

What the day cycle cannot do here. The art has its lighting baked in — the map is painted with a fixed sun, and a house sprite is a front elevation with its shading already in the pixels. So the cast shadows move and the overall colour moves, but the shading on the buildings themselves does not. At dawn a house is lit warm from every side at once. Fixing that properly means either separate sprites per lighting direction or a normal map per prop, and the earlier relief experiment on this map is a reason to be cautious about the second.

Patching the ground underneath

Each structure's pixels are replaced with the most common terrain colour found in a ring around it, rather than one flat fill for the whole map. A house on sand gets sand, one on the olive grass gets olive. The seam is still visible if you look for it in mode C — real art would paint what is underneath rather than infer it — but it is good enough to judge whether the standing version is worth pursuing.

What this does not settle

  • Whether it should ship. Nine objects on one small map is a good showcase and a weak sample. The question is what it looks like on a map with a hundred.
  • Sorting. These are drawn far-to-near with no depth buffer, which is fine while structures never overlap. They will overlap on a denser map, and λ and g both make that happen sooner by giving every sprite more screen height.
  • Whether the day cycle belongs on the world map at all. It is built here because it was asked for and because it is the honest way to ground a standing sprite, but a map whose art has a baked sun fights it, and travel time being a resource means the clock would need to mean something in the simulation too.
  • The party sprite. These are world-space and shrink with distance. The engine's marker is fixed screen size. One of the two has to give.
