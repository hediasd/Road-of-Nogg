Clouds over temp2

Replacing the procedural cloud shadows with the supplied art: two cloud shapes and two ground shadows. A cloud is a horizontal sprite at altitude, so it rides the same mode-7 scanline pass the ground does — one ray per screen row, intersected with a plane at cloud height instead of at zero. Its shadow lands on the ground by the same shear the buildings use: altitude × cot(sun elevation), opposite the sun.

Two things the art says that are worth knowing before choosing anything. There is no transparency in it. 55,872 fully clear pixels, 9,664 fully opaque, zero partial — so any softness has to be applied at render time rather than read from the file. And the two shadows are different colours, both from temp2's own palette: 08696a is its shadowed sea and e69900 its shadowed sand. That is not a translucent overlay, it is shadowed terrain — the same thing the building shadows arrived at by snapping to the palette. Which means a fixed-colour shadow sprite is only right over the terrain it was drawn for, and the Palette mode below is the one that generalises.

Settled, 2026-09-04, in the engine. Both judgement calls this sketch raised were confirmed once the art was actually measured: the sheet is 8 px blocks under a 1 px outline, which pins the display scale at exactly native — the Size slider below does not survive that finding, and the shipped control takes a whole multiple of native instead. And each shadow is drawn by hand with its own foreshortening (shadow B is a full row shorter than its cloud), not derived from the cloud by a scale factor, so Spread is gone too. Darkening the ground and snapping to its palette reproduces the artist's own two shadow colours exactly at shade 0.25–0.50; 0.40 is the default. One thing this sketch could not show and the engine now can: at a close framing (Curved Close, Closer) a single native-scale cloud spans 30–50% of the frame's width, which reads as intended weather at Tile-Exact and as a cloud eating the map up close. A cloud is a horizontal sprite, not a billboard

The map is seen from above at a steep pitch, so a cloud lies flat like the ground rather than standing up like a building. That makes it the same problem the ground already solves: one ray per screen row, intersected with a horizontal plane. The only difference is the plane's height — camera_height − altitude instead of camera_height. Clouds are therefore perspective-correct for free, and they take the same curvature the ground does, so they stay parallel to it rather than sliding across it as the world bends.

It also gives parallax for nothing. A cloud and the ground beneath it land on different screen rows because their rays hit at different distances, so panning the camera slides the cloud against the map by exactly the amount its altitude earns.

The shadow is the building shadow's shear, reused

A cloud's shadow lands altitude × cot(elevation) away from it, opposite the sun — the identical relation the standing structures use, with altitude in place of building height. So the shadow moves with the time of day, the two stay locked together as the wind blows, and a cloud directly overhead at noon sits almost on its own shadow while one at 07:00 throws it far to the north-west.

The shadow is drawn into the same map-space mask the building shadows use, at the same resolution as the region art. Its edge therefore lands on map pixels and cannot crawl when the camera pans, which is the whole reason that representation was chosen.

What the supplied art settles, and what it does not

Measured: zero partial alpha. Every pixel is either fully clear or fully opaque, so the Opacity and Softness knobs here are applied at render time and are a decision, not a property of the file. Softness dithers the shadow's edge in map space rather than blurring it, for the same reason the building shadows do.

Also measured: the two shadow sprites are 08696a and e69900, which are temp2's shadowed sea and shadowed sand. The artist drew shadowed terrain. That is the same answer palette-snapping reached independently for the building shadows, and it is why Sprite mode looks right over one terrain and wrong over the next while Palette mode works everywhere.

The art's colours are a hair off the map's

Worth fixing at source, and easy to miss by eye. Four of the five colours in the cloud sheet are almost temp2's, and not quite:

Only the orange lands on the palette. The rest read as the right colour and are not it, which is how a seven-colour map quietly becomes an eleven-colour one. Measured on the ground alone, with the clouds themselves excluded: Sprite mode puts 42,410 pixels outside the palette and Palette mode puts zero. That is the cheapest argument for snapping, and the cheapest fix is to correct the five values in the source PNG.

The near-miss sea colour #31aeac appears on both the clouds and the shadows — it is the one-pixel outline around each shape. So the outline was meant to be the map's own sea and is not, which will show wherever a cloud crosses the coast.

What this replaces

The shipped ground shader carries cloud_strength, cloud_scale and cloud_speed, driving a procedural noise field. It is off in every preset. The Noise option reproduces it for comparison: it is cheap and it never repeats, but it has no shape anybody drew, no cloud above it to explain it, and no altitude, so it cannot parallax and cannot move with the sun.
