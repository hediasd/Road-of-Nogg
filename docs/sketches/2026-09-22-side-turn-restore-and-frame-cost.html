Teardown and rebuild, not reversal

The judgement call was whether a restored side turn should unwind what the screen had drawn or throw it away and redraw. Reversal would oblige every future effect to define an inverse, and the first one that forgot would leave the board lying. These two frames are two rendered frames apart, across one restoreSideTurn().

Disposing the adapter bumps the visual queue’s serial, so a tween completion or watchdog still in flight for the abandoned timeline finds a serial that no longer matches and does nothing. That is what keeps a late callback from advancing the restored timeline.

A slice count is the wrong budget for a caller that owns a frame, because slices are not equal: enumerating one actor costs milliseconds while pre-scoring a batch costs a fraction of one. Four expensive slices landing in one frame is what produced the 27 ms outliers. Where a slice falls cannot change the decision, so spending a wall-clock budget instead is a pacing choice rather than a search one.

Not fully solved. A single enumeration slice can still overshoot the budget on its own, which is the remaining 17–20 ms tail — roughly one dropped frame per side decision at worst. Removing it means subdividing enumeration below one actor, which is a larger change than this cycle took on.
