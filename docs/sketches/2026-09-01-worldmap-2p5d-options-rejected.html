World map 2.5D options

Trees and mountains are painted into the region texture, so they lie in the ground plane and take the same perspective squash as the terrain. At the Overland framing's 40.5° pitch that is obvious — a mountain reads as a stain. This page renders the same view eleven ways so the options can be compared against the real map rather than described. Start with J. The earlier options are kept because the ones that fail are worth seeing fail.

Verdict: all of it rejected, 2026-09-01. Eleven ways of giving the world map depth were built and compared against the real region art. None was kept. The map stays flat, which is what the console references it is modelled on actually do. This page is preserved because the failures are specific and worth not repeating — each option below carries why it failed, and three of them failed for reasons that were only visible once rendered, not from reasoning about them.

The region art is embedded no longer. Load a region PNG with the file input to drive this page; it opens on a procedural placeholder otherwise.

What each option costs

  • A — Flat. What ships today. Baseline for comparison.
  • B — Contact shadows. An ellipse under every prop tile, in the ground layer. Not 2.5D at all, but it is the cheapest thing that stops props reading as stains, and standing props will need shadows anyway. One art pass, no code, no new data.
  • C — Billboard props. Superseded by J, and kept to show why it failed: it cuts sprites on the 16 px tile grid, but these objects are 10–40 px and do not align to tiles, so it slices straight through them. Trees become upright quads standing on the ground. In the game this is one MultiMeshInstance3D per atlas and one instance per tile, so thousands of trees cost one draw call. Fixed yaw is what makes it cheap: nothing ever rotates, so there is no billboarding maths and the artist draws each prop once in a fixed three-quarter view rather than eight directions.
  • D — Extruded tiles. Rejected: quantising organic mountain blobs to the tile grid turns them into a staircase of cubes. Each tile gets a height and becomes a box, textured on top by the existing art. Mountains get true silhouette and occlusion. Billboarding a mountain reads as a cardboard cutout because they are wide; extrusion does not. The asset is one pixel per tile — a 48×64 greyscale image for this whole region.
  • E — Both. Props billboarded, landforms extruded. The recommendation.
  • J — Decomp billboards. The one that works. Magnify the region art and every object on it is already drawn in oblique view with a top and a front: each mountain has a pale lit crest above a dark body, the fort has a light roof over a battlemented wall, the trees are conical and lit from one side. That is the same structure GBC tile art has, and it is what the Pokémon decomps exploit — standing such a sprite up shows it at roughly the angle it was already painted for. Each object is found by connected-component search, erased from the ground, and stood upright anchored where its base met the floor. Its own pixel height becomes its world height, so a mountain stands taller than a tree with no authored per-object data. In the engine this is one MultiMesh, one draw call, and no billboarding maths at all, because the fixed yaw means a quad already faces the camera.
  • K — Decomp + relief. J with G's lighting applied to the ground it stands on.
  • F — Parallax offset. No geometry: the ground shader offsets its texture lookup along the view vector by a height value. Free, and it degrades exactly where it is needed — shallow angles. Overland is its worst case. Included so the failure is visible rather than argued.

The gotcha to design around first

Anything that stands up has to bend with the ground. Overland uses curvature — y = -k·d² applied in the ground shader's vertex stage. The moment props become separate meshes they must apply the identical bend, or they sink into the ground near the horizon and float in the mid-field. Raise Curvature k here with option E on: the props follow because this page applies the same displacement to them. In the engine that means curvature stops being a ground-shader detail and becomes a shared world-space function every world-map mesh includes. Cheap now, painful to retrofit.

Decisions this forces

  • Sprite scaling. Props here are world-space, so they shrink with distance. The party marker in the engine is currently fixed screen size. At Overland's 2.06:1 depth ratio the party would read wrong against the far trees. One of the two has to change.
  • Alpha handling. Use alpha-scissor, not blend, so props write depth and sort against each other correctly. It is also the right aesthetic call — no soft edges in pixel art.
  • Fog must apply to props identically or they punch out of the haze. Another reason to factor fog into a shared include alongside curvature.
  • Height couples to gameplay. If terrain gains elevation, roads painted on flat ground no longer sit on it and the node graph becomes height-aware. Worth deciding whether terrain height is purely cosmetic or something roads must respect.
  • The art has to be split. This page fakes it by colour-key. Really shipping C, D or E means trees and mountains leave the ground layer, or you see both copies. That, not the code, is the real expense.
