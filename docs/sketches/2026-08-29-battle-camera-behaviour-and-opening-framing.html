The battle camera:
where it opens, what it follows

Two questions, one sketch. Where does the battle open? — explored live below, because that is a comfort judgement no formula settles. And what is the camera doing at every moment of a battle? — written out as tables, because the camera today has four hooks, one dead switch and no written contract, and every future argument about it should be an argument with a table rather than with a file.

Almost nothing here is implemented. The tables are the target behaviour; the last column of §4 audits what the code does today, and it is mostly empty on purpose.

§1

What the board already decides

Four facts constrain the opening shot before taste gets a vote. Three of them point at the same answer.

FactWhere it comes fromWhat it forces
Yaw 0 already faces player 2 At yaw 0 the camera sits at +Z and looks down −Z. World z is grid y; team 1 deploys at high y, team 2 at low y. “Behind the player 1 models, facing the player 2 models” is yaw 0. No new angle needs inventing. The shipping camera simply sits 11.3° off it, for no recorded reason.
Aiming snaps to quadrants _board_direction_for() rotates arrow keys into board directions through nearestQuadrantYaw(), and the director settles to an exact multiple of 90° before a player turn opens. A resting yaw that is not a multiple of 90° makes that mapping an approximation rather than an identity. Of the four legal quadrants only yaw 0 looks at the enemy. That is the whole decision.
The armies are diagonal All three maps deploy team 1 at low x/high y and team 2 at high x/low y — opposite corners, not opposite edges. At yaw 0 your army opens bottom-left and theirs top-right. The board reads as a clean rectangle; the fight reads as a diagonal across it. Facing straight down the army axis instead (yaw −63.4° on Meadow) centres the fight and turns the board into a diamond. One or the other.
The projection is orthographic PROJECTION_ORTHOGONAL; size is the view height in world units. There is no perspective convergence to sell “low, looking down”. A low pitch only squashes depth, by exactly sin(pitch) — at 30° the board is half as deep as it is wide. The cost of dropping the camera is measurable here, not a matter of taste. Camera distance, by the same token, does nothing at all to the image.

The current opening angle was never chosen. _setup_camera_and_lighting() places the camera at (6, 15, 14) while focus_point is still the placeholder (8, 0, 4), and _ready() derives the spherical angles from that pair: yaw −11.31°, pitch 55.77°, radius 18.14. The battle then overwrites focus_point and size once the board dimensions are known — but not the angles, which keep the placeholder’s values for the whole match.

So the opening angle is the residue of two hard-coded vectors that were never meant to survive contact with a real board. It is also why the grid sits very slightly askew on screen, and why default_size — snapshotted at the same moment — still holds 14.0 rather than the battle framing, which is what a double-middle-click reset returns to.

Shipping

Yaw −11.3°, pitch 55.8°, size 15.55. Nearly top-down, slightly skewed, and loose: on Meadow the same angle fits the board at 11.22, so the frame is about 39% larger than it needs to be. Square the yaw to zero and it is worse still — 58% × 43% of the frame. It reads as a map rather than as a battlefield.

Tabletop — try this one first

Yaw 0, pitch 38°, fitted size. Square to the board, plainly above it, plainly looking down. Depth squashes to 0.62, which still leaves a 16×8 map reading as a rectangle rather than a strip, and it is low enough that models have some presence against the ground.

Low

Yaw 0, pitch 24°. The most dramatic, and the first angle where a model in front hides the tile behind it. Worth looking at, probably not worth planning a turn from — but a strong candidate for the establishing shot that then rises into the resting angle.

Army axis — rejected

Straight down the team 1→team 2 diagonal. Genuinely over your own army’s shoulder, and genuinely the wrong shape: the board becomes a diamond and the yaw is not a quadrant, so arrow-key aiming stops being exact. Kept because the rejected option is half the record.

§3

What the camera does, moment by moment

The states are exclusive and ordered by priority: the lower a row sits, the more it outranks the rows above it. Subject is what the camera is centred on, motion is what it does on its own, and owns is which rule from UI_DESIGN.md §9 applies — position-only, or full authorship.

StateEntered whenSubjectMotionOwnsLeaves on
Establish Board dimensions are known, once per battle Board centre Optional: hold a wider, lower angle for ~1.2s, then ease to the resting angle Director First turn opens
Planning Player turn opens; Phase.MENU The acting unit Still. Settled on a quadrant. Director Phase leaves MENU
Aiming MOVE_SELECT / TARGET_SELECT The grid cursor Follows the cursor, position only, edge-triggered — it moves when the cursor nears the frame edge, not on every keypress Position only Confirm or cancel
Confirming CONFIRM_ACTION Actor and target Still, framed so both are visible Director Commit or cancel
Following A movement visual action starts The moving unit Tracks the unit with a short lag; never rotates Position only The move animation ends
Deliberating A CPU turn opens The acting CPU unit Slow orbit, ORBIT_DEGREES_PER_SECOND, at the resting zoom Director Playback drains
Casting A cast animation starts with radius ≥ CAST_FRAME_MIN_RADIUS The affected area’s centre Widens to fit the area plus margin. Never zooms in. Director Playback drains
Handover Playback for the turn drains Whoever acts next Stop orbiting, settle to a quadrant, reframe at the resting zoom Director Next turn opens
Resolved The battle ends The winning side Slow orbit, no settle — nobody aims from here Director Scene exits
Player driving Any input handle_input() claims: wheel, middle-drag, right-drag, reset Whatever the player wants None of its own. Everything above abandons immediately. Player Reasserted at the next turn boundary, not mid-turn

Two rows carry all the risk. Aiming is the one that will feel bad if it is done naively: a camera that recentres on every cursor keypress turns the board into a treadmill. Edge-triggered follow — the cursor moves freely inside a dead zone, and the camera only pans when it approaches the frame edge — is the difference between a helpful camera and a nauseating one. Following is the one the current code has no verb for at all; see §5.

The director is switched off, and nothing can switch it on. BattleCameraDirector.set_enabled() has no caller anywhere in the project. _enabled is therefore false for the life of every battle, and all four of its hooks return on their first line — except on_player_turn_began(), whose free-mode branch reproduces the old “pan only if the unit is off screen” behaviour.

So the orbit during CPU turns, the resting zoom, the cast pull-back and the quadrant settle are all written, all documented in UI_DESIGN.md §9 as though they ship, and none of them has ever run in a battle. Any camera work starts by giving that switch an owner — a graphics-menu toggle, defaulting on.

§5

The verbs, and the one that is missing

Every behaviour in §3 has to be expressible as a call on BattleCameraController. Five verbs exist; the state table needs a sixth.

VerbTouchesShapeRule
panFocusTo()focus_pointOne-shot tween, 0.35sPosition only. Never rotates or zooms, so it can be used against a view the player set.
frameTo()focus_point, sizeOne-shot parallel tweenTakes authorship. Moves and zooms; still never rotates.
orbitBy()current_yawContinuous rate, integrated in _processThe only verb that rotates. CPU turns and the post-battle state only.
settleToQuadrant()current_yawOne-shot tween to the nearest 90°Must complete before any aiming phase opens.
snapshot/restoreFreeView()All fourCapture and one-shot tween backWhat makes taking authorship reversible.
followTarget() — missingfocus_pointPer-frame, until releasedEvery existing verb tweens toward a fixed value. A unit sliding across three tiles is a moving target, so the “Following” row of §3 cannot be written with any of the five above — a frameTo() fired at the start lands where the unit will be while it is still elsewhere. This wants a callable evaluated in _process next to the orbit integration, with the same lag behaviour, and released on the animation’s end.

Also worth noting: no verb moves pitch. Only the player can, by middle-dragging. If the establishing shot is going to rise from a low angle into the resting one, pitch needs to join the tweenable set — and then restoreFreeView() already handles giving it back, because it snapshots pitch too.

§6

One camera, or several?

The instinct is right — the behaviours really are distinct enough to want separate objects. The question is whether those objects should be Camera3D nodes.

ApproachHow a switch looksCost here
Several Camera3Ds, swap current An instant cut Three concrete problems. The status badge rows and mouse picking both read camera.unproject_position() and project_ray_origin() off the one camera the controller holds, so every reader would need to learn which camera is live. nearestQuadrantYaw() must have one authoritative yaw or arrow keys change meaning on a cut. And a cut is exactly wrong for the content: a tactics player is holding a mental map of the board, and a hard cut costs them that map every time the cursor moves.
One camera, several behaviours A state change; the camera keeps moving continuously Recommended. This is what BattleCameraDirector already is, half-built — it just has four entry points instead of a state machine. Each row of §3 becomes a small object that answers “where should the focus be, what size, what yaw rate”, and one place applies the winner. Everything downstream keeps its single camera reference.
One camera, weighted blend of rigs Rigs fade in and out; the result is a weighted average What Cinemachine does, and overkill here. The states in §3 are mutually exclusive by design and their transitions are already handled by tweens. Revisit only if two subjects ever need framing at once — and the “Confirming” row solves that case with a midpoint instead.
A second camera for cuts only Deliberate cut, then back The one case that genuinely wants a second node: a KO or finisher cinematic, where the cut is the effect. Not needed for any row in §3, and cheap to add later precisely because it would not be sharing state with the main rig.

§8

Still open

  • Does the board flip for player 2? Yaw 0 is “behind team 1”. In a hot-seat or PvP battle, either team 2 plans from behind the enemy, or the resting yaw becomes 180° on their turns — which is a quadrant, so it is legal, but it is a 180° rotation between every turn.
  • Does the establishing shot exist at all? It is the one state in §3 that is pure presentation. Cheapest version: open at the resting angle and skip it entirely.
  • Does the camera follow the cursor, or does the cursor stay inside the camera? §3 assumes the second. The first is simpler and worse.
  • Who owns the director switch? A graphics-menu toggle is the obvious home, but a camera mode is not really a graphics setting.
  • Pitch during an orbit. A drifting camera that also rose and fell slightly would read as alive rather than mechanical — and would be the first VFX-style layered oscillator applied to the camera. Also the easiest thing here to overdo.
